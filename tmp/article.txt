Protein synthesis is the fundamental process by which individual cells build specific proteins based on instructions stored within their genetic code. Proteins perform virtually all essential functions in living organisms—acting as structural scaffolding, enzymes, signaling molecules, and transporters. The core pathway of protein synthesis follows molecular biology's "Central Dogma": DNA is copied into messenger RNA (mRNA), which is then decoded to form a polypeptide chain.

Phase 1: Transcription (Reading the Master Blueprint)
In eukaryotic cells, the genetic blueprint—DNA—is safely stored inside the cell nucleus. Because DNA cannot leave the nucleus without risking damage, the cell creates a portable copy of the required gene.

Initiation: An enzyme called RNA polymerase binds to a specific region on the DNA strand known as the promoter. It unzips the double helix to expose the gene's nucleotide sequence.

Elongation: RNA polymerase reads one strand of the DNA (the template strand) and synthesizes a single strand of complementary messenger RNA (mRNA). Instead of thymine (T), RNA uses uracil (U) to pair with adenine (A).

Termination: Once the enzyme reaches a stop signal, the newly created precursor mRNA detaches, and the DNA strands rewind.

Processing: Before exiting the nucleus, the pre-mRNA undergoes modifications: non-coding segments (introns) are spliced out, leaving only coding segments (exons). A protective cap and tail are added to ensure stability. The mature mRNA then travels through a nuclear pore into the cytoplasm.

Phase 2: Translation (Building the Protein)
Once in the cytoplasm, the mRNA strand attaches to a ribosome, the cellular machinery responsible for assembling proteins. The ribosome reads the mRNA sequence in three-base groups called codons. Each codon specifies one of 20 distinct amino acids.

Initiation: The ribosome binds near the start codon (usually AUG, which codes for the amino acid methionine). A specialized molecule called transfer RNA (tRNA) brings the corresponding methionine amino acid to the ribosome. Each tRNA carries a three-base anticodon that complements the mRNA codon.

Elongation: As the ribosome moves along the mRNA strand codon by codon, successive tRNA molecules deliver matching amino acids. The ribosome forms covalent peptide bonds between these incoming amino acids, linking them into a growing peptide chain.

Termination: Translation continues until the ribosome hits a stop codon (UAA, UAG, or UGA). No tRNA recognizes these sequences; instead, a release factor prompts the ribosome to release the completed polypeptide chain and disassemble.

Post-Translational Folding and Processing
A newly synthesized chain of amino acids is not yet a functional protein. To work properly, it must fold into a precise three-dimensional structure. Helper proteins called chaperones assist in folding the chain into specific helices, sheets, and intricate tertiary loops. Some proteins undergo additional chemical additions—such as attached carbohydrates or lipids—in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus before being dispatched to their final destinations inside or outside the cell.
